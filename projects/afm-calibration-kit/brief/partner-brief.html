Calibrating AFM with DNA origami: an open method, with its uncertainty

dobladillo project · 29 September 2026 · open source (EUPL-1.2), self-hosted tools only
0 nmheight, as an AFM draws it20 nm

An AFM image of anything smaller than 100 nm has no good ruler to be checked against: commercial pitch gratings stop at about 70 nm. DNA origami puts features at designed positions a few nanometres apart, and it can be imaged in the same buffer as the sample. The objects already exist, some of them for sale. What does not exist is an open, validated way to turn an image of them into calibration values with an uncertainty. That is what we propose to build, and where we would like partners.

Already exists

Origami AFM samples are sold: a ruler rectangle and a tip-reconstruction staircase (GATTAquant). One metrology institute has published an AFM study (VTT MIKES, 2017). None carries certified values.

Open

Open software that reports scale, skew, tip, resolution and drift with uncertainties; AFM traceability of origami dimensions; a reference sample built for high-speed AFM in liquid.

What we ask

Instrument time and images, one traceable reference measurement, and eyes on the method. Details in section 7.

1The calibration gap below 100 nm

An AFM's lateral scale is set against a pitch grating. The smallest commercial ones are around 70–150 nm pitch, and a field about 100 nm wide cannot be calibrated with a grating much coarser than that [Dixson 2010]. Atomic lattices (mica, graphite) work at a few nanometres, but small ambient scans are dominated by drift [Kim 2022]. Between those two lies the scale at which proteins and DNA nanostructures are imaged, and most of it is calibrated by extrapolation.

The tip is the other unknown. It broadens every feature by several nanometres, it wears during a session, and it is usually characterised on a separate hard sample, not in the image that is being measured. High-speed AFM has no reference sample at all; laboratories improvise with collagen, streptavidin crystals or graphite [Marchesi 2021; Umeda 2023].

DNA origami is a natural candidate for both. Its features sit at positions set by base-pair counts, it deposits alongside the specimen, and it images in liquid. Its weakness is equally clear: its dimensions have been reported to vary by 3–10 %, "too high for a proper metrological calibration standard", most likely because of deposition and drying rather than the structures themselves [Graugnard 2017], and its apparent height depends on imaging force and buffer. It can be a transfer standard, calibrated once against a traceable reference; it is not a primary one, and it is not a height standard.

4The proposal: a method, not another sample

The kit has four parts. The software comes first because it fills the clearest gap, needs no laboratory to start, and works with samples that can already be bought.

PartWhat it is
Softwaredobladillo calibrate (working, validated on synthetic scans): reads a scan of any reference whose geometry is declared (ours, GATTAquant's, Kolbeck's) and reports lateral scale, skew, tip shape, resolution and drift, each with an uncertainty. Open source, runs locally.
Reference samplesA calibration tile: a DNA-origami rectangle carrying a sparse, chiral pattern of hairpin marks whose centres are the ruler, all DNA, readable whichever way it lands. A six-helix bundle of about 380 nm as a long-range length and width reference.
ProtocolFolding, purification, deposition and imaging specified tightly enough that the 3–10 % preparation spread is measured and bounded, not assumed away.
Certificate methodHow a batch's dimensions are tied to a traceable reference, with a GUM-style uncertainty budget. Until then every output carries traceable: false.

Why the scale can be separated from the sample

Origami tiles land at random angles. The scanner's distortion is fixed in the image frame, while the tile's own anisotropy (its helix spacing, which depends on buffer) turns with each tile. Fitting many tiles at once therefore separates the two: the x and y scale and the skew of the scanner, and the true helix spacing of this preparation. The software refuses the fit when the tiles' angles are not varied enough to tell them apart.

Two findings from this stage shape the plan. The tile's size across the helices cannot be taken from the design, because published helix spacings range over 2.25–2.6 nm; the fit therefore measures it, and the x/y ratio and skew do not depend on it. And a staircase of flat blocks gives blind reconstruction nothing to work with, since a flat top with sloped sides is consistent with any blunter tip; it constrains the tip through its declared geometry instead. The published staircase work reports blind reconstruction from real images, so experiment K4 tests this on real data.

Every value is reported with its standard uncertainty and a k = 2 interval. Scans are read in their native formats (Bruker, JPK, Asylum, Gwyddion) through the open AFMReader library. No real image has been analysed yet: that is K3 onwards. Not built yet: a report that reads a whole experiment folder at once, high-speed AFM movie formats (frames are exported one by one), and grating pitch analysis (Gwyddion does it).

7What we ask of partners

  • Instrument time and images. Scans of the tile and the bundle in air and in liquid, with the instrument's own calibration record, so the software can be tested against something real.
  • A high-speed AFM session for K6: frames of the tile in buffer over tens of minutes.
  • One traceable measurement for K8, from a laboratory with a metrological AFM or a traceable grating at the relevant scale.
  • Review of the method: the uncertainty budget, the choice of criteria, what an AFM user would need the report to say.

In return: the designs, protocols and software are open (EUPL-1.2), results are shared with contributors, and authorship follows contribution. Nothing depends on an external service; every tool runs locally.

8Limits we state up front

  • Not a height standard. DNA's apparent height depends on force, mode and buffer; the kit can report it, not calibrate against it.
  • Not traceable until certified. Gratings and metrological AFMs reach parts in 104; uncertified origami is good to a few per cent.
  • Preparation matters. The 3–10 % spread is the main risk. The protocol and K5 exist to measure and bound it.
  • Not a competitor to existing samples. The software reads commercial samples too; its value is the method and the uncertainty.
